Follow one turn

Mira opens with two cards and declares 2. Arun sees her claim. What reaches his phone depends on what happens next.

Mira plays two cards

  1. Her browser sends the move. play_cards carries the selected card IDs and opening declaration. Her actual hand is already known to the server.
  2. Render checks it under the room lock. The connection must own Mira’s seat. It must be her turn, the cards must be distinct and in her hand, and the declaration and last-card rule must permit the move.
  3. The room sees the claim. Render moves both cards into the pile and broadcasts cards_played with the number claimed, count, pile size, and doubt deadline. Arun sees “This round 2” and “Mira added 2 cards to the pile.”
  4. Each screen gets its own update. Only Mira receives her revised hand_updated. The room receives the next turn and updated public counts. Arun can now play, pass, or challenge while the window is open.

Arun calls doubt

  1. The challenge arrives. call_doubt targets the latest play when the server handles it. The first eligible challenger wins the race for that room.
  2. Render compares the two cards with 2. If they are a 2 and a 7, Mira takes the whole pile. If both are 2s, Arun takes it. Arun earns one doubt toward his quota either way.
  3. Both players see the evidence. doubt_resolved reveals just those two cards. The pile recipient privately receives their new hand. On a caught bluff, the browser also plays the MP3.
  4. The next seat opens. Unless the play ended the game, the player after Mira chooses the next round’s number. In this two-player example that is Arun.

Arun’s phone reconnects

  1. The browser pauses its controls. During a game, Render holds the seat for 30 seconds. If it was Arun’s turn, Render pauses his turn clock.
  2. The phone presents its saved credential. reconnect_player includes the room code and session token. Render validates the seat and expiry. A successful takeover disconnects the previous socket for that seat.
  3. Render rebuilds Arun’s view. It sends his private hand, the turn and roster, then seat_sync with the pile, remaining doubt window, disconnected seats, any current public reveal, and the result if the game ended.
  4. Play resumes from server time. The browser enables controls after synchronization. An expired seat is removed and its hand discarded. If only one player remains, that player wins by forfeit.

Who sees what?

The backend chooses the recipient for each event. Private cards stay out of other players’ browser data as well as their screens.

Room-wide

Names, public player IDs, readiness, connection status, card counts, doubts made, the declared number, turn and pile counts. A challenge reveals the values of the challenged play.

One seat

The owner receives their card IDs and values. The browser sorts the hand for display. The room code, seat ID and reconnect token are saved locally. The device also remembers its name and hint preference.

Server memory

All hands, the ordered seats, hidden pile, latest play, passes, pending win, connection mappings, deadlines and room locks. One process owns these together. A restart, redeploy, or idle shutdown loses active tables.

From lobby to last card

Expand a stage to see the rule, the event that carries it, and the boundary that keeps it consistent.

Creating a table and dealing the deck

The host enters a name and sends create_room. Render returns a five-character room code, player ID and private session token. Friends use join_room with the code. Only the host can change lobby settings through update_settings. All joined seats must send player_ready before a game of 2–4 begins.

Cards are numbered 0–10. Random mode defaults to 12 cards per player and allows a total of 10–25 times the player count. Seats get equal hands and leftovers are discarded. Fixed mode uses four or five copies of each number and deals all 44 or 55 cards around the table. game_started announces the order. Each seat receives its own hand.

Turns, passes and burning the pile

The first seat in join order opens. The opener chooses one number and plays one or more cards face down. That number stays locked for the round. Later players can add any number of their own cards under that claim, bluff, or send pass_turn. Play continues clockwise, including back to the opener.

Each new play resets the pass count. Once every remaining seat has passed since the latest play, including the person who played it, round_burned discards the pile. Round openers rotate in seat order after both passes and doubts. If A declares this round, B opens the next, then C, then A again at a three-player table. An opening timeout or an allowed opening pass hands the declaration to the next seat, and rotation continues from whoever actually declares.

Turn clocks, doubts and winning

Each turn has 30 seconds. An opening timeout advances the opener. Later timeouts count as a pass. A doubt closes after five seconds or when the next valid play or pass arrives, whichever happens first. The previous player cannot challenge their own play. Each room has its own lock so simultaneous challenges resolve in order.

The default quota is two doubts made, adjustable from zero to ten in the lobby. A player who still owes doubts cannot empty their hand during ordinary play. A one-card opener who owes a doubt can pass immediately. If the pile is empty and every remaining player has one card and owes doubts, the opener may play their last card with the usual challenge window.

A final play that survives wins. A caught final bluff returns the pile to its player. A truthful final play wins even when challenged. game_over ends the game for the entire table.

Session recovery and room cleanup

Each socket is bound to one seat, and only the active socket may act for it. On disconnect, an in-game seat has a 30-second grace period. A lobby seat is removed immediately and the host can transfer. The browser resynchronizes after reconnecting or returning to a visible tab. The local countdown uses timing supplied by Render.

A sweep runs about once per minute. Empty rooms expire after ten minutes and finished games after sixty. Invalid room lookups avoid allocating room state. The v3 guard allows up to 512 stored rooms and defaults to 120 entry attempts per peer per minute. Those values are admission limits. Simultaneous playable capacity depends on server resources and traffic.

Inside each player’s browser

App.jsx switches between Home, Lobby and Game. useRoomSocket.js listens for state changes and sends player actions. Hand, Pile, PlayerList and DeclareBar render what that seat can see. DoubtButton and RevealModal handle the challenge window and result. Optional instructions appear on the active player’s screen.

Netlify or the Render static frontend delivers HTML, CSS, JavaScript and the reveal audio. The game page also references Google Fonts. The production bundle reads VITE_SERVER_URL at build time. Socket.IO can use HTTP polling and upgrade to WebSocket. Changing the backend URL requires rebuilding the frontend.

Running and testing the same architecture locally

The Vite development server runs on port 5173 and proxies /socket.io to Uvicorn on port 8000. Opening separate tabs with ?seat=1 and ?seat=2 gives each a distinct local session so two players can share one computer.

The automated tests cover the rules, real socket flows, race conditions, seat takeover, reconnects, room limits and private-hand delivery. To check both frontends together, create a room on Netlify, join from the Render frontend, play a card, and refresh one browser. Both players should still see the same pile.

Operating a single in-memory server

The container binds to Render’s assigned port. /health checks that the service answers HTTP. A two-seat playthrough also checks browser origin configuration, the socket connection, and delivery of private and public events.

The production origin allowlist covers both frontend sites. Origin checks and seat credentials serve different purposes. The former restrict browser origins. The latter determine who may resume or act for a seat.

Reading and updating a small room in RAM avoids a database round trip on every turn. The process is also the recovery boundary. A saved browser token can reclaim an existing seat only while its server-side room survives. Running additional independent workers would require shared room state and coordinated event delivery.
